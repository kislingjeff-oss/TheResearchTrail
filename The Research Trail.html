The Research Trail

Every quotation in One Acre in the Heart of the City came from one place: Jeff Kisling's own Journal Research Desk, a free archive of everything he's written since 2015. This page retraces exactly how those words were found — so you can use the same searches to discover the next story yourself, whether it's about the Hub or something else entirely.

Why this exists. The storybook is one story out of thousands sitting in Jeff's archive — this page is the map for finding the others. Rather than just describe the research, it reproduces it: the exact words typed into the Desk's search box, what came back, and the post each quotation was traced to. Follow the same moves on your own question, and the Desk will turn up its own story.

Before you search — get to the Desk

Every step below starts from the same place. Here's how to get there.

  1. Open tinyurl.com/Journal-Research-Desk — it lands you directly on the Search tab, already selected.
  2. Click into the search box at the top of the page.
  3. Type your words. Put quotes around a phrase, like "Urban Resilience Hub", to find posts with that exact wording, in that exact order. Leave the quotes off, like mutual aid Iowa, to find posts that contain all of those words somewhere, in any order — a looser net that catches more posts.
  4. Press Enter, or click the green Search button.

The search box — type here, exactly as shown, then press Search.

The Journal Research Desk holds every post from Jeff's eight blogs, 2015 to 2026 — 3,244 posts, 1.8 million words. Nothing is excerpted or summarized; it is full text, searchable by phrase.

Casting the net, then narrowing it

Five searches, each run live in the Desk's own search box, so anyone can repeat them and expect the same result. The method is always the same two moves: first a broad search on the subject itself, to see the whole cluster of posts it touches; then, for each quotation the storybook actually uses, a narrow search on that quotation's own distinctive wording (or the post's title, when that was more exact), until the search returns one post — the post — rather than a shelf of near-misses.

The one real snag
Jeff posts to more than one blog, and a post's cross-posted copy can carry a later date than the original. Embodied Land is the clear example here: the Desk's search list shows it under Channels Into Knowledge, dated Aug 7, 2026 — but opening the post itself shows a byline of June 23, 2026, which is when Jeff first published it on Active Objection. The storybook cites the original date. The rule this trail confirms: the search finds the post; only opening the post itself confirms when it was actually written.

What the Desk can't do

The Desk searches text — every word of every post — but it doesn't hold photographs. The ten images in the storybook and infographic came a different way: by looking through Jeff's own picture library by hand, folder by folder, choosing the ones that matched what the words already said. That step has no search box. It's a person looking at photos and recognizing a moment — the demolition, the digging, the eagle overhead, the crew at the end of a long week.

Built for youth groups: filtering by age

This matters as much as the search box itself — the Desk was built with Sunday school classes and youth groups in mind, and every one of its 3,244 posts carries a suggested age range.

Each post shows a badge — All ages, Ages 12+, Ages 14+, or Ages 16+ — and, when it touches a hard subject, an orange line naming it, like "Mature themes: deaths of children."

  1. On the Search tab, find AGE in the filters on the left.
  2. Set it to the age of the youngest person in your group — for example, "Youngest is 12 or 13."
  3. Only posts suitable for that age are shown, and they're added to Selected & Export automatically.

How the age is set. Each post gets the higher of two measures: its reading level (sentence length and word difficulty — this alone never raises a post above 14+), and its mature themes — suicide or self-harm, sexual violence, graphic violence, genocide and war, the deaths of children, police violence, drugs, and strong language. A passing mention raises the age a little; a post centered on the subject raises it more.
Please read before sharing
These ranges are guidance worked out by a computer, not an official rating. Read a post yourself before sharing it with young people.

Turning a search into a reading list

Every search's matches land automatically on the Selected & Export tab — this is how you turn an afternoon of searching into something you can hand a class.

  1. Run your search, then open Selected & Export. Everything that matched is already checked in.
  2. Remove anything you don't want with its ×, or start over with Clear all. Give the list its own title.
  3. Pick a format — Rich text, Markdown, Plain text, or CSV — and choose what to include: a summary of each post, its blog and date, tags, age range, and more.
  4. Check the live preview, then Copy the list or Download it as a file.

Format, what to include, a live preview — then Copy or Download.

The summary line the Desk exports for each post is not a machine-written description — it is the opening sentences of the post itself, in Jeff's own words. A reading list built this way is still, start to finish, his writing.
